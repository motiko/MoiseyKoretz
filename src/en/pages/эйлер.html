Euler

When the great mathematician Euler, the astonishing number of whose works still amazes anyone beginning to study higher mathematics and mechanics, came to Russia together with his daughter, who was to him both secretary and nursemaid, to work at the Russian Academy of Sciences then being established, his fame already resounded throughout the scientific world. For four decades the Bulletin of the Academy of Sciences was filled mainly with his works, and the complete collection of his mathematical and mechanical writings, recently published, consists of 80 full-weight volumes.

He lost his sight suddenly. Waking at his usual hour and opening his eyes, he saw nothing. It was June, and from the Neva a coolness came in through the open window. He thought clearly that in a white Petersburg night it is never so dark, not for a single hour. The great wall chimes melodiously struck six.

Calmly and distinctly Euler three times repeated aloud the terrible truth that had opened before him.

Having dressed by touch, he carefully, so as not to make the furniture clatter, went into the other room and, having washed neatly and thoroughly, made his way in the same way, by touch, into the study to his writing desk.

In the familiar armchair it was cozy and safe. For several minutes he sat in silence, with deep pleasure drawing the fresh air of the summer morning into his whole chest.

"Lizchen, - he called his daughter, -Lizchen, from this time on we shall work together. Sit opposite me and read in my manuscript the last lines written by me yesterday."

And for another twenty years the mighty and lucid mind of the great blind man set and solved problems of genius, and to this day we apply his solutions whenever we build bridges, canals, houses.

He died in his working chair at the age of 85.

Despite the ever-growing weakness and the tormenting pains in his stomach, not for a single day did he break off his intense, titanic labor."

27 November 1946
