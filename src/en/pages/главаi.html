Chapter I

The hot sun of Egypt

Rose in the blue sky

And with scorching rays

Flooded the valley of the Nile.

Everything was as in the pictures:

A pensive sphinx by the temple,

Bas-reliefs, strictly in profile,

Pyramids in perspective.

And, of course, there were likewise

All the animals and birds,

Those which, according to Brehm,

Are obliged to live in Egypt.

In the large foreground

Half-naked Jews

Knead clay, cut straw,

And carry bricks off to dry.

Foremen from among the Jews

Watch over the work

And shout every minute:

"Come on, come on, fellows!"

And the Egyptian receiver,

Gone soft under the awning,

Takes the bricks by a strict norm

In stacks.

On an enormous papyrus

The tally of the work by brigades

Was kept by a clerk from among the Jews

In a dirty-white garment.

Rabbi Zkharya ben Reb Zorakh

(It was he who was the clerk),

Fortunately, a rare art

Possessed, of writing and reading.

He had even studied counting

By the Babylonian system,

And knew how to do addition

Up to five thousand and more.

And so our ben Zorakh,

Though he was a poor Jew,

Did not knead clay with his feet,

But held a papyrus in his hands.

Still, among the elders

And the other Jews of rank,

Who had managed to place themselves

In the Egyptian service,

He had no authority,

Since it was known to everyone

That Reb Zkharya was an exceedingly

Impractical man.

On this day, from morning, the Jews

Talked a great deal,

Which, of course, was reflected

In the success of production, -

Because in that epoch

The Jews could not yet,

While holding a talk with anyone,

Keep from waving their hands.

"Have you heard, Rabbi Zkharya" -

Seized the scribe by the hem

Out of a deep pit

A black-bearded foreman, -

"Have you heard that there has returned

From the desert the son of Amram,

Brother of the elder Aron,

The famous Rabbi Moyshe?

That by some miracle

Pharaoh's daughter raised him,

And he has brought from the desert

A remarkable god,

Specially for the Jews.

And now for the Jews the norms

For brick will be cut in half,

And of millet and lentils

The issue will be doubled!"

"Ts-ts-ts! But is it true?

Rabbi Zkharya doubted -

And besides, what connection is there

Between norms and a god?"

"May I, on this very spot,

Be butted by the black Anis,

If today you, ben Zorakh,

Have not gone clean out of your mind!"

Suddenly she stuck in her own word,

The cook, Aunt Dvoyra,

An energetic Jewess

With breasts beyond all measure.

"All the market women in the bazaar

Spoke with one voice,

That with the coming of Moses

We shall all become masters.

As for Pharaoh's daughter, -

Well, everyone has long known

That by Moses

She has already borne a child.

I myself heard of it

Last autumn in Memphis,

From such an eyewitness

As will not speak one false word.

Pharaoh, in a terrible rage,

Ordered Moses to be drowned,

And Moses through a little window

Fled then into the desert.

But now, when the child

Will soon be fifteen,

And another heir

Amon has not sent to Egypt,

Pharaoh has forgiven Moses

And has decided to appoint him

Forever first minister

To the heir of the throne.

But for the present this whole affair

Is kept in the strictest secrecy,

And to Moses, for a guard,

They gave a god from Memphis."

At this point Rabbi Zkharya

Wanted to ask Dvoyra

How Pharaoh's secret

Had become known so easily.

But it is far easier, in a storm,

To sail a boat against the wind,

Or, in the days of the Nile's flood,

To cut its course with a dam,

Than to utter a single word

In a conversation with Aunt Dvoyra,

Of which there had long been convinced

Her husband, Natan ben Ezra.

And besides, it flattered Dvoyra

That with their mouths wide open

The Jews standing round

Greedily caught at her speech.

Even Sherlock Holmes could not learn

How many reliable facts

Dvoyra would have managed to report,

If she had not been interrupted.

Someone sharp-eyed among the workers

Unexpectedly saw

That in the distance, by the very huts,

Near the Jewish settlement

Which stretched in disorder

From the brick factory

To the Egyptian quarters,

Rabbi Moyshe had appeared.

And as if by a simoom,

A hot wind from the desert,

All the Jews were blown at once

Off in the direction of the settlement.

And clutching at those who ran,

So as not to be left the last,

Shaking her bulk,

Dvoyra ran off with them.

The Egyptian receiver himself

Almost rushed after them,

But then, with a wave of his hand,

Sat down again in the shade of the awning,

Picked up an old papyrus,

Shook some makhorka into it,

And having rolled a papyrus, *)

Drew on it with pleasure.

(They say that before Columbus

No one smoked in the Old World, -

But what fool will believe

These old wives' tales.

Well, of course, not brazenly,

Not before a wide public,

But they smoked in secret

From the historians of culture.)

Rabbi Zkharya ben Reb Zorakh,

As always, fallen behind them all,

Past the crowd that had gathered

Could see nothing at all.

He only heard a roar of rapture

And shouts of welcome,

And saw how Moses

Was carried in their arms into the settlement.

But while, with the crowd of workers,

He was making his way back to the factory,

He learned far more

Than he could have seen himself.

"Did you see, did you see" -

Plucked at his arm,

Spraying saliva thickly,

The black-bearded foreman -

"Did you see how Reb Moyshe

Struck the ground with a stick,

And out of a huge crack

Two snakes crawled at once?"

"What lies are you telling, Reb Halevi"-

They interrupted the foreman -

"It was the staff of Aaron

That Reb Moyshe turned into a snake.

And then that snake swelled up

And burst with a terrible noise,

And with the snake's white fat

Moses smeared his hand".

"Moses smeared nothing at all,

But the wonder-worker's hand

Turned white with leprosy

And was healed at once."

The argument over what Moyshe had done

Would soon have turned into a fight,

If at that moment, toward the factory,

There had not drawn near a litter

With a faded canopy,

Under which there sheltered

Yellow, bilious, and angry,

A strict senior overseer,

A former junior priest of Isis,

Transferred for drunkenness

From the capital, Memphis,

To the brick factories.

Under his master's gaze

Work boiled up again;

The foremen and the receiver

Got ready for fault-finding.

And in silence Rabbi Zkharya,

Who had unrolled the papyrus again,

Noting the course of the work,

Thought with bitterness and pain:

"Can Rabbi Moyshe be

Simply a visiting conjurer,

Who has rolled in on tour

To us in the godforsaken provinces,

To put on an old trick

That has bored everyone in Memphis,

Where it no longer takes in a penny,

The turning of a stick into a snake".

But catching himself in this

Subconscious tirade,

Rabbi Zkharya burst out laughing,

To the surprise of the workers.

He was laughing at himself

And at the unexpected bitterness: -

"So I did believe, after all,

The chatter of fat Dvoyra!"

The sun was slowly sinking

Behind the Libyan Desert,

But to the valley of the Nile the evening

Brought no fresh coolness.

Zkharya, who had already supped,

In the yard, under an old palm,

Gently stroked the head

Of his beloved daughter Naomi.

Naomi's black brows

Curved more sharply than a bow

Above thick lashes

And gazelle eyes.

Like a gazelle was Naomi,

Swift-footed and slender;

Her breasts, just beginning to show,

Like firm apples

That a mischievous boy had hidden

From his father's supervision,

Were already plain to see

Under a loose summer dress.

And at thirteen Naomi,

Among the Jewish girls,

Was marked out by her beauty

And was the first of the brides.

And she alone in the family

Understood the rabbi's jokes

And softened the irony

With laughter joyful and ringing.

And when she laughed -

The world became more beautiful,

And in Rabbi Zkharya's soul

The bitterness melted in an instant.

And Naomi very often

Ran away from her girlfriends,

So as, pressing her father's large palm

To her tender cheek,

To listen to long stories,

Peppered all through,

About the wondrous world of thought

And the stupidity of the universe.

Often there joined

Them an old friend of Reb Zkharya,

Dvoyra's husband, Natan ben Ezra,

Who served as a telegraphist

At the central telegraph.

(I would ask you, reader,

Not to laugh at this point,

Because in the ancient world

There was already a signal telegraph.

You, of course, could not have

Sent: Love Kiss

Wait tomorrow in the city of Thebes

Adoring Ramsesik".

For such a telegram

There would not have been signals enough;

But serious reports: -

About a flood in the upper Nile,

Or that on the border

An enemy had appeared, -

Were delivered more exactly

Than happens in our time.)

And though Natan ben Ezra himself

Was a noticeable man -

"Dvoyra's husband" was what

All the neighbors usually called him.

Dvoyra's husband was the constant,

Irreplaceable partner

Of Rabbi Zkharya in that pastime,

Cunning and serious,

Which served in place of chess,

Unknown in that epoch,

So that intelligent men

Should have no time to think.

And like all telegraphists

(By the canons of all novels,

Regardless of the century).

In his young years, with a guitar -

Oh, pardon me, I slipped - with a harp.

With a broad pink bow

He charmed his Deborah, -

And then Deborah the harp -

Broke it across his back.

And then Natan ben Ezra,

Become from then on "Dvoyra's husband",

Like all telegraphists,

Regardless of the century,

Became a profound politician,

Discussing every decision

Of Pharaoh's ministers,

And a partner of Rabbi Zkharya.

"Reb Natan, sholem aleykhem -

Rabbi Zkharya half rose,

To greet with a bow

Natan, who had come up.

"Have you heard, Rabbi Zkharya,

Of today's events?" -

Instead of any greeting

Dvoyra's husband at once began.

- Is this about Moses?

- What strange questions!

And what else can the Jews

Talk about today?"

"I must confess, I do not understand

This strange figure,

And all this talk

About some new god.

Without that, already in Egypt

It is crowded enough with gods, -

Here, wherever you turn,

You bump into a god."

"You, Reb Zkharya, are a blasphemer.

All these are false gods.

There is one god in the world,

The God of Abram and Isaac.

And it was this very god

That Moses met in the desert,

And the Lord directed him

To put the gods of Egypt to shame."

Rabbi Zkharya burst out laughing:

"This is no simple matter!

For the Egyptian gods

Are no toothless fellows.

Well, and if they have

A hard time of it against the new god,

Then the Egyptian army,

Without a doubt, will help them.

And the police, of course,

Will not stand for a newcomer

Driving out all the ancient gods

From the territory of Egypt."

Dvoyra's husband was a little abashed

By such an argument.

"Yes. With the police, perhaps,

It is harder for a god to cope.

And, of course, it will be better

If he, leaving the disputes aside,

Takes the Jews away with him,

Farther from the police."

"If only Rabbi Moyshe

Managed to lead the Jews away,

I should be ready to believe

Even in a donkey's hoof!

For our misfortune is not this,

That heavy work

Upon the herdsmen of Israel

Pharaoh ordered to be done;

And our misfortune is not this,

That we see little meat,

And that Jewish dwellings

Are not adorned with carpets, -

But the most terrible misfortune

Is that the children of Israel

Have made their peace with a slave's life

And have forgotten freedom."

"Rabbi Zkharya, you are a dreamer

And a naive freethinker,

And of real politics

You do not understand the first thing.

In historical science

This has become an axiom, -

That a sober politician always

Relies upon a god.

Look at Ramses

And at the other pharaohs, -

Their success was determined

Only by a close bond with a god.

To me, of course, it is unknown

What Rabbi Moyshe has in mind,

And what sort of god

He has brought with him to Egypt, -

But now the Jews must

Expect extraordinary miracles,

And, of course, a change

In their legal position."

Convinced long since

That the streams of eloquence

In an argument with Dvoyra's husband

Only Dvoyra could stop, -

Rabbi Zkharya argued no further,

But sent his Naomi,

Who, with her eyes opened wide,

Was catching every word,

For the playing board,

And, having set out the pieces,

Made an intricate move,

So that Reb Natan ben Ezra

Fell silent in mid-word.

  1. *) Our "papirosa", as the author thinks, is clearly a corruption of the Egyptian word "papirusa", from the name of the material out of which the ancient Egyptians rolled goat's legs.
