Letter twenty-three

June 1952. The letters of this period have not survived. In March 1952 the term of imprisonment ended, and Father passed to the status of an exile. Fearing a second imprisonment (which happened not seldom in those terrible times), he moved still farther north, beyond the Arctic Circle, to the settlement of Inta, Komi ASSR. I, having finished university, am at last preparing to go to him. Here is an excerpt from a letter:

... In July and in August the chief of my cardboard party will be on leave and I shall be taking his place, so that most of the time I shall be in the settlement (that is, I shall come back to it to sleep, since by day I shall have to go to various points some 10 -15 kilometers off) and only now and then shall I go away for two or three days. In short, Skripka (warehouse manager, who is also the commandant and the transport manager) will know everything and will tell you what to do. If I am long in the field, he will bring you to me by motorboat or with a drag sled. You will leave your things in my dugout or with Skoipka. Bring with you: 1. A dress and shoes for dancing (here at the club they often dance) or for the cinema. 2. The same for volleyball (there is a splendid gymnasium). 3. For bathing (if you are not afraid of icy water). I have prepared ski trousers for you (they are floating in the dugout just now, but I shall dry them). I have not yet been able to buy rubber boots for your foot; we shall have to ask Skripka (he has some for women in the warehouse, but he hides these boots. I hope that when he sees your photograph, he will set a pair aside for you). Do not bring money for the journey back, but reckon all the same so that on arrival in Inta you have 30 - 50 rubles left, in case you do not find me at home. Watches cannot be bought here, but there are briefcases. In short, I am saving now, so as to squander it with you later, when you are here. The library here is very good; on my card there are two books, so if you are not roaming the tundra with me, you will be able to read. In short, come. I think you will not be bored. I have received the certificate and the diploma. (Of graduation from the Leningrad Polytechnic Institute). Thank you for carrying out my request so quickly... Just now we no longer have nights (not even white ones, of the Leningrad kind) and shall not until the end of July. So when you arrive, there will be no need to walk in the dark. You wrote that you have a graduation banquet on 5 July. If you are not going to miss it, then I expect you on 9 July.

Do you like to fish? There is a great deal of fish here in the tundra lakes, and our party has boats. There are nets too, but if you fish with a rod, then bring hooks with you hooks. I cannot abide fishing, nor gathering mushrooms, but when I was starving I learned both. My comrades are preparing for the catch with enthusiasm, and I have stipulated one grayling for myself from the catch and do not wish even to lift a finger. Whoever likes it, let them feed others with it , and I, short of extreme need, take no interest in these pastoral occupations. ( The correspondence went on for 10 years. I had not seen Father for 14 years. And so, furnished with a comparatively recent photograph of him, I got on the Leningrad - Inta train and climbed up onto the third bunk. I step out onto the platform. It is light, a white night. In my hand a photograph of Father; I recall Mama’s description: a gait a little bit to one side. No one about. A man coming toward me: Tell me, is this the train from Leningrad? It is he. We sit down in a little local carriage. I feel shy. How am I to address him, with ty or with vy? And here is the dugout, already a little dried out after the spring flooding. And...surprises. But the correspondence naturally broke off.-E.K.) ...
