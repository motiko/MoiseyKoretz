Letter to a grandson

My dear grandson, Misha!

Shalom!

Thank you for describing your studies. In childhood I managed to learn only the Humash (the Pentateuch - we were taught to know it by heart) and Neviim (the Prophets). After that my studies were secular in character (historical fiction and plain history). And from the age of 13 I stopped my studies outright, and I laid tefillin for only about two weeks. And I became an atheist from about the age of 8, though I loved the rites and until about the age of 12 I went to the synagogue.

I am answering your question about our forebears. Alas, I too know only about the grandfathers. My grandfather on my father's side moved from the town of Korets to the shtetl of Talnoye, in the Uman uezd of Kiev Governorate, and married there. (Which came first, I do not know. It is possible that the marriage was the reason for the move.) I have heard that there was some famous Rebbe fun Talne, but that was not my grandfather. My grandfather was a weigher – a receiver at the mill, and my father told me that the peasants who brought grain to the mill greatly respected him for his honesty and his fairness. In the shtetl they called him Reb Moyshe fun Korets. Therefore, when Alexander III ordered that surnames be given to all the peasants and the shtetl Jews, the police officer wrote him down as Moyshe Koretsky. Evidently he died before I was born, and so they gave me his name. It seems that his patronymic, too, was ben Reb Avrum.

In 1881 (it turns out, exactly a hundred years ago) my father was born, and he was named Avrum–Yitzhak. When at 15 he left for Odessa to be taken on as an apprentice to a watchmaker, the police officer gave him a passport in the street nickname Abram Moiseevich Korets. And so, only in 1896, the surname you bear came into being. On the day of my bar mitzvah I was called Rav Moyshe, ben Reb Avrum- Yitzhak.

My grandfather on my mother's side arrived in Sevastopol already married. His name was Reb Lipman Eisurovich (his brothers were written down with the surnames Usherovich, Osherovich, so that his father was evidently called Usher). He died when I turned 1. He had three daughters (among them my mother Slava (Simkha), Aunt Nyunya (Anna), whom Ira knows, and Grandmother Zori, Sofya, and 6 sons. His wife, my grandmother, was named Esfir. Your mother was named in her honor. All 9 of the children grew up; no one died in childhood. The sons – Mikhail, Boris, Yosef, Israel, Solomon, David. Boris Eisurovich was some kind of public figure in Palestine.

I have two sisters - Tatyana (in honor of my great-grandmother Traina) and Lipa (in honor of Grandfather Lipman, though the police officer wrote her down as Lyubov. She has died) And some 12 -15 cousins. I know all their names, but I have information about by no means all of them.

Besides that, my father had a sister, Feiga, who went on living in Talnoye. She and the greater part of her descendants (she had four children) perished in the German occupation – killed by the Nazis. In particular my cousin Lazar and his children. And that is my whole genealogy, as far as I know it. Of course I still remember many details. If anything interests you, I will write.

I wish you success in your studies and in your spiritual development. All of us, though we are not Orthodox, and are even atheists, are grateful to you that you pray for us "the Shema and so on."

Shalom, your grandfather Moshe Korets.
