Letter five

/11/44

Mezhog, Sangorodok Protoka.

My dear little daughter! I feel a tremendous guilt before you! Here before me lie three letters of yours, for which I simply could not find the time to answer. There was a large piece of work which had to be finished, no matter what, by the first days of this month.

I do not remember whether I wrote you that at our infirmary there is a scientific research laboratory for the study of vitamin-deficiency diseases, attached to Sevzheldorlag. In this laboratory I have now for two months been a research worker, in the post of statistician-mathematician. Strange as it may seem, even in confinement my education has been saving me for five years now, almost always sparing me the need to get closely acquainted with a crowbar, a pick, and a shovel.

(Every morning Father was taken out of the camp and led under guard to the infirmary, and in the evening brought back.)

זWhat my work consists of, I shall write you after you let me know whether you know what vitamins are and what role they play in the life of a living being.

For now I shall answer your first letters. From your letter of 3/9 I want to dwell on two thoughts that express one and the same feeling from different sides. The first: I, so you say, write uninteresting letters, but I shall learn to write interesting ones, for I have only finished 7 grades so far.

– First, your letters are without any doubt interesting to me, however you write them and whatever you write about. Whether you write of small details of daily life or of your thoughts, all this, for me, is you, my daughter, whom I love, and every feature of whose life is full of interest for me. The only thing I do not like is, forgive me, a certain pomposity in your letters, an unnecessary effort to make them interesting.

Write more simply, straight on, whatever comes into your head; write any old how, but only more sincerely and more simply. From now on I shall catch you at pompous expressions and write to you about them, so that it will be easier for you to be rid of what in books is called provincialism.

Second, not one of your spiritual shortcomings can be excused by your having finished only 7 grades. A person can and must be spiritually whole quite apart from his formal education, as measured by the grades he has passed. And it is not good if your development and your education go only along the channel along which the school directs them. I wrote to you once, a year ago, that a person can take shape only by thinking for himself and by meeting people who think. And books supply the questions to think about. So, little daughter, do not excuse yourself with your seven grades, and if you see intellectual shortcomings in yourself, then demand of yourself what one demands of a finished person.

You have read a great deal, but have you thought a great deal?

The second: I want so much to learn and to see, but I shall still be in time.- Again you are making excuses, but this time not with grades, but with years. And that is not right. Already now you must know what you want, and every minute go toward it.

- True, the conditions of these years are extremely unpleasant. But this year is the last year of the war, and after it ends there will be no excuses left for you and me if we do not invent a way to satisfy your longing to see.
