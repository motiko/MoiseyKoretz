Chapter II

If you, reader, in childhood

Ever managed even once,

Having run off from papa and mama,

To poke a stick into the bees' hive, -

Then you, of course,

Will at once be able to picture

How the Jews behaved

In the days when Moses came.

The most trustworthy rumors,

In a hundred different versions,

And stories of eyewitnesses,

Each ruling out the others,

Appeared every hour,

So that soon Rabbi Zekharya,

Trying in vain to believe them,

Was utterly at a loss.

Some insisted that Rabbi Moyshe

Would demand, in Memphis,

That there be founded in the Nile delta

A state of Israel;

Some told how Moyshe

Wanted to lead the Jews

Out to some wondrous island,

Where on the palm trees

Pies grow, complete with filling;

Some whispered in a friend's ear.

Having shut the door more tightly,

That next week

Moyshe would become pharaoh...

But the rumors agreed in one thing:

Whatever Reb Moyshe might demand, -

Pharaoh stubbornly

Answered him in everything with a refusal,

Not wishing of a sudden to lose

Labor that cost him nothing,

Which was indispensable

For the construction plan,

And the stubborn Rabbi Moyshe

Is now bringing God into play,

So as by miracles

To frighten the Egyptians nearly to death.

And in everything now the Jews

Managed to see the plagues

Which, in reprisal upon the Egyptians,

Rabbi Moyshe was sending down.

Whether locusts had swept down

Upon the fields of the middle Nile,

Or the scabies had been brought

By Numidian nomads,

Or the downpour was too heavy,

So that the Nile broke the dams, -

The Jews explained it all

By the miracles of Moses.

And in vain did Rabbi Zekharya

Hint to them, cautiously,

Shrugging, out of habit,

His slightly stooped shoulders,

That phenomena such as these

Had often recurred before, -

The stubborn ones, the skeptic's words,

Would not even listen to.

But all the same, displeasure

At this untimely skepticism

Gradually accumulated

Among all his acquaintances.

And you yourselves, reader,

Had you found yourselves with them,

Rabbi Zekharya's behavior

Would have thought unseemly.

Indeed, - people believe

In what it is pleasant to believe,

Even if this belief

They invent on the spot themselves.

And besides, everyone knows

That pleasant hopes

Possess an important property

Of improving the digestion, -

And so among optimists

Diarrhea comes less often

Than among cold skeptics

And tearful pessimists.

Unfortunately, Rabbi Zekharya

Did not take all these facts into account,

And, one day, the displeasure

Burst out in indignation.

At any latitude in the world,

In the marshy lowlands

Flooded by the overflow,

There is a great quantity of midges.

Oh, mosquito, lord of the taiga!

How much innocent blood

You drink up every year

From the people subject to you!

They say a certain scholar

Has calculated that so much blood

Man has not yet spilled

In all the wars there have been in the world!

(But still, there is hope, -

Notes this same scholar, -

That with the technical progress

Of weapons of extermination,

Adapted to the growth

Of the human herd as well,

Wars will be able to outdo

The records of the mosquitoes.).

Those brickworks

Where the Jews labored

Were situated, as ill luck would have it,

Close by one such lowland.

And when the time came,

Myriads of insects

Swept down upon the workers,

To torment them and to sting.

This was a punishment

Worse than the work itself

Beneath the scorching rays

Of the burning sun.

"Phew!" exclaimed Reb Halevi,

Plastering his cheeks with clay,

So as to save himself, if only briefly,

From the bites of the insects.

"All the years I have lived on earth, -

Never yet have I seen

So much accursed midge

As has come down today."

Rabbi Zekharya at once recalled

That for some fifteen years, perhaps,

Reb Halevi, every year,

Repeats this very phrase.

But the work brigades,

From the habit of agreeing

With every opinion of the foreman,

Confirmed in a united chorus

Halevi's statement.

And, of course, this was

Not mere toadying,

As Rabbi Zekharya thought,

But their sincere opinion.

For all of us have found

That when a mosquito bites -

It is a hundred times more painful

Than all of last year's bites

"Yes" - said Reb Zekharya, in jest, -

Even Moses could not invent

A worse plague for the Egyptians

Than the bites of these midges."

In that brigade there were many

Young enthusiasts,

Blindly believing every rumor

Of the victories of Moses.

And of those terrible punishments

Which Reb Moyshe calls down,

So as to break the stubbornness of the malicious

Intimates of Pharaoh.

And no sooner had there reached their ears

The fashionable word of a plague, -

Than conditioned reflexes

At once suggested the conclusion:

"Undoubtedly, - this is Moyshe,

Who has summoned the midges into Egypt,

So as to compel Pharaoh

To let the Jews go the sooner".

And faster than a telegram

There ran through all the brigades

The rumor that Rabbi Moyshe

Had again sent a plague upon Egypt.

And into Reb Halevi's brigade,

From every end of the works,

People came, so as in detail

To ask about the new miracle.

And the details, of course,

Were not slow to appear -

Down to the very day of the talks

Between Pharaoh and Moses.

Astonished, Rabbi Zekharya

Saw how there had come

Out of his chance joke

A trustworthy report.

And at once he became

Very much ashamed that, without meaning to,

By his absurd joke,

He had led the Jews astray.

To set the matter right,

Zekharya stood upon a flat stone

And to the people gathered there

Addressed a loud speech:

Respected brothers!

What I said about the plague

Was, brothers, only a joke,

I confess - not a very clever one.

Where is the logic, Jews, -

In sending midges into Egypt,

If these midges sting

Us more than they sting Pharaoh?

And in general, even the meetings

Of Pharaoh with Moses,

Of which so very much

The Jews are saying now,

Could not even have taken place, -

Since the whole of the past month

Pharaoh spent with the troops

In distant Ethiopia,

As of this, officially,

Announcement was made everywhere,

So that all this is an absurd..."

Here indignation burst out!

A frantic cry arose,

Drowning the rabbi's speech, -

In the general noise it was impossible

To make out a single word.

Rabbi Zekharya only saw

Faces red with anger,

And opened wide,

The mouths of the indignant Jews.

Long they beat Rabbi Zekharya,

And would have beaten him to death,

If good Reb Halevi

Had not brought order with his stick.

But one hot-headed brawler

Would not obey even the stick,

And when Halevi

Began to drag him off by the legs -

He, squealing with excitement,

Bit Zekharya on the ear

And with his teeth gnawed off

The left earlobe clean away.

But spitting out the blood,

Decorated all over with bruises,

The rabbi stubbornly repeated,

Hardly moving his lips: -

"And where is the logic, Jews? "

For two weeks Rabbi Zekharya

Lay ill in his bed,

But even lying there he managed

Not to fall behind the course of life.

Strange though it was, not the slightest

Offense did he feel,

Or malice toward the Jews

Who had beaten him for nothing.

The beating itself, though,

From a philosophical point of view,

Seemed to Rabbi Zekharya

Exceedingly interesting.

Dvoira's husband, Natan ben Ezra,

Dropped in every evening,

So that the news of the day

He might tell his sick friend.

Gradually there emerged

The direction of events: -

Rabbi Moyshe, on the advice

Of Yehoshua ben Reb Nun

(Who as Jesus Navin

Is called in Russian books).

Without waiting for permission

From Pharaoh's Ministers,

Was making ready, on his own,

To lead the Jews away with him,

And the elders to this

Had already given their consent.

And Rabbi Yehoshua himself -

A young commander

Of Pharaoh's infantry,

And a splendid strategist besides:

A brilliant career

He had made in Pharaoh's army,

But the other day he had resigned

Because of the antisemitism

That reigns in the military caste.

Yehoshua maintains

That now, in view of the war,

The garrisons in the rear

Have grown extremely weak.

And no one now will be able

To prevent the Jews from leaving

The hateful bondage,

The Egyptian captivity.

"Now this is something I can hear,

And not a miracle with the mosquitoes -

Rabbi Zekharya brightened

On hearing this news.

"Sha, you stubborn freethinker," -

Ben Ezra interrupted him -

"You, I see, will not be taught

By any scuffle".

"You are right" - said Reb Zekharya,

And felt the lobe of his ear -

"To doubt a miracle nowadays

Is very bad for one's health.

But I think that later on,

In three thousand years,

One will be able to live in the world

And not believe in miracles".

Some ten days, roughly,

After these conversations,

Naomi once came home

In an unusual attire:

A dress of red byssus,

Gilded sandals,

Turquoise earrings,

Combs of ivory,

And heavy bracelets

On her wrists and her ankles -

Like a wondrous frame, they set off

The beauty of the slender dark girl.

And Rabbi Zekharya could not help

Admiring his dear daughter,

Flushed, and shyly abashed,

By the feeling that had wakened

And by the unclear sensations

(For the first time, perhaps, in her life)

Of womanly beauty and strength,

Of the fearful power of charm,

Which, in Solomon's opinion,

Can become stronger than death.

(Solomon, on this question,

Is an authority for me: -

He had three hundred concubines

And six hundred lawful wives) *)

"What is the matter, my Naomi?

For what festival have you dressed?

And where did you get

These costly things?"

"Do you not know, Papa,

That tomorrow morning

Rabbi Moyshe is leading away

All the Jews from Egyptian captivity?

And that God has commanded the Jewish women

To borrow from the Egyptian women

Things, clothes, and dishes,

So as to keep them afterward?

I asked Aida **)

The one whose garden borders ours,

To give me this dress for a day".

- So!" - said Rabbi Zekharya,

And his face in an instant

Darkened, turned gray,

As if covered by a cloud.

And covering his face with his hands,

Turning away from Naomi,

The rabbi smiled bitterly

And thought: - "Idiots!

Even while doing a holy thing,

The most perfect of deeds, -

People simply cannot manage

Not to foul the road as they go!"

But Naomi, unable

To think too abstractly,

Understood that Rabbi Zekharya

Was grieved by what she had done.

And herself already near tears,

With all her little strength

She tried to pull away

Her father's hands from his face.

Papa, Papa, dear Papa!

But I didn't know, Papa dear,

That you would be displeased

That I had borrowed a dress.

And I myself would not have gone

And hurt our Aida,

If there had not been, for this,

Permission from God!"

Seeing the great tears

In the eyes of his Naomi,

Rabbi Zekharya came down

From the clouds to earth again.

"Now, don't cry, my Naomi!

You are a good girl, I know.

But, daughter, one should not listen

Sometimes to other people's advice.

I do not know about the others,

But it seems to me, Naomi,

That theft - is a bad business,

Even with God's complicity.

Still, I am not going to argue,

And I am not going to command, -

You decide yourself, little daughter,

What we are to do with this dress."

Without a moment's hesitation,

Like a little goat, Naomi,

Having skipped quickly out of the house,

Went tapping with her little heels

Along the trodden path.

And some ten minutes later,

Back to Zekharya, who was smiling,

Naomi returned once more

In her shabby old dress.

A little breathless from running,

She looked at her father,

And her face was shining

With the light of an idea grasped.

Until the dawn Rabbi Zekharya

And the beautiful Naomi

Sat through this night

In the yard beneath the old palm.

And it seemed to them both

That in all the great Egypt,

Dozing under the moon,

There were no people happier than they.

At daybreak, up to their little hut,

There rolled up, thundering,

A two-wheeled cart

Harnessed to a camel.

"Rise up, old skeptic!

Gather your things, and quickly!

Today we are all shaking off

The hateful dust of Egypt from our feet!"-

Cried Natan ben Ezra,

Who in the kindness of his heart

Had found the time to see

To transport for his friend as well.

And from the great joy,

That the dream had been fulfilled,

Of the exodus of Israel

From Egyptian bondage,

The old skeptic - Rabbi Zekharya,

Freethinker and dreamer,

covering his face with his sleeve,

Wept like a child.

  1. *) For more, see the well-known poet S. Frug.
  2. **) Try as the author might, he could not recall a single Egyptian woman's name, except the title of Verdi's well-known opera, - although according to the text of the opera Aida is not an Egyptian but an Ethiopian.
