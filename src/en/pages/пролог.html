Prologue

Time, in a furious torrent

Flows across the universe

And carries off beyond return

Much joy and much grief.

Together with the furious torrent

We rush on through life,

And in our haste do not notice

How the bank slips by.

But today, for a moment,

I decided to step out onto the bank,

And all wet and beaten

I clambered onto a shore stone.

Ah, some stone again?

Now look. Stubborn Pegasus,

- If you gallop into the ravine again,

Then you shall taste the whip too!

On the path of allegories

You will lose every horseshoe, -

You had better turn

Onto the broad road.

It was a late April evening,

Outside the window a blizzard moaned...

(I ask you, do not smile, -

Not everywhere is it spring in April).

For the sake of warmth and of quiet

Having curtained the little window

With a thick blue blanket,

I sat in my little cabin,

Sprawled in a comfortable chair,

At my favorite table,

Quietly taking pleasure

In solitude and in peace.

These rare minutes

Of an unaccountable quiet

Are sent as a reward

For the sufferings of the soul.

I was afraid even to think,

Lest with the edges of a sharp thought

The silence should chance to cut

Its own palms.

These warm palms,

That lay upon my forehead

And gently smoothed away

The deep wrinkles of the forehead.

Only to the soft, barely audible

Memories of bygone days

Did I permit a stroll

Along the long avenues of the brain.

I grew up a cosmopolitan,

And then, as if to order,

Became a zealous patriot

And a Slav of the old stock,

But today, for some reason,

(An unaccountable desire!)

I wanted to be again

As I was born - a Jew,

"Can it really be unclear?" -

The silence whispered in my ear -

"You have forgotten, surely, dear,

That today

Is Erev Pesach".

Passover evening!

The first seder!

Thirty years have passed, I should say,

Since that time when a Passover evening

I kept for the last time.

And, seated at the end of the table,

In a ringing, trembling voice,

For the consolation of all my elders,

I would ask the "arba kashoys",

That is, I would ask the questions,

The famous four:-

How the Passover evening differs

From all other evenings.

On the table the candles flickered

With little golden tongues,

A pleasant smell came drifting

From the open door of the kitchen,

Everyone had grown somehow kinder,

And more dignified, and calmer, -

For an ordinary prank

No slap on the neck threatened me.

By all of this it was set apart,

For me, the Passover evening, -

But in answer to the "arba kashoys"

Everyone was expected to hear out

The endless Haggadah,

That ancient legend

Of the exodus of Israel

From distant Egypt.

( Though this time the Haggadah

Did not seem long to me, -

A fat volume of Gustave Aimard

Had been hidden beforehand

Under the table for this occasion).

My teacher, the old rebbe,

Used to say that Jehovah

Very much likes the Jews

To recite the Haggadah aloud.

The wise Rabbi ben-Akiba,

Who was in ancient Palestine,

Once repeated it

Eight times to his household!

And when all the household

Had fallen into a frantic snore

(Not having thought to bring,

Evidently, a volume of Aimard), -

The rabbi ran out of the house

And ran along the unpaved

Streets of Jerusalem

And caught the late passersby,

So that, for a lesson to the revelers,

He might tell the Haggadah again,

Of the exodus of Israel

From far-off Egypt.

Seeing that it was no robber,

But only a decrepit old man

Who had buttonholed him, the passerby

Hastened to get away.

But no such luck!

The wise Rabbi ben Akiba,

With a firm, experienced hand,

By two buttons at once,

On the ancient lapserdak

Of the man who was fleeing, caught hold.

(I, of course, do not vouch

That in those days in Palestine

Everyone wore lapserdaks:

Perhaps they wore togas,

Or various chlamyses, -

Still, there were buttons!).

In one night, some twenty times, ben-Akiba

Managed to tell the Haggadah: -

Twenty different buttons

Were left in his hands!

Gold, bone,

Wooden, and there even

Were some torn out "with the meat"

From a best suit.

The old man did not keep them long

And donated them for a memorial,

As a holy relic,

To the great Temple of Solomon.

But while, forgetting the book,

I was recalling ben-Akiba,

Papa, Grandfather, and Uncle Duvid

Had got through half the Haggadah.

And had already come to the song

With the rollicking tune,

Which is written in the folk

Aramaic tongue.

This little song - "khad gadye" -

About a scruffy little kid,

For which two zuz

Were paid at the market.

Whether two zuz be much or little,

I do not know even now,

But at that time a zuz seemed to me

Something like three kopecks

(Three kopecks - that is money!

For them neighbor Fyokla

Of white pumpkin seeds

Poured me two pocketfuls! )

Musing on the little kid

That had contrived to make its way

Without trouble into the holy books,

I slept through the end of the Haggadah.

And woke only from the noise,

From the merry congratulations

And the mutual wishes:

" L'shono bo b'Yerusholaim"

"L'shono bo b'Yerusholaim" - -

In a year, in Jerusalem!

For how many thousands of years have the Jews

Repeated this phrase!

And it seemed, only give it,

Only give them the chance,

And seventeen million

Will fling themselves at once toward Zion.

But when the late Balfour,

For the sake of cunning combinations,

Declared to the Jews

A return to Palestine, -

The stiff-necked Jews

Fell to scratching the backs of their heads:

Better, said they, a tomtit in the hand,

Than a crane in Jerusalem.

And in the thirty years that passed

There gathered of them in Palestine

Fewer than half a million, -

And for this Jehovah

Set the fascists upon them!

At the latest Assembly

They decided, with finality,

That the Jews had Palestine

Slept through once and for all.

Evidently, in our time,

There was no Moses to be found,

Who by a miracle or by the whip

Might lead them to Canaan,

As this is expounded to us

By our ancient Haggadah...

Time, time! You are all-powerful,

You bring changes,

Forever, in a furious torrent,

You flow across the universe

And carry off beyond return

Much joy and much grief.

So, in my secluded little cabin,

I mused upon the past,

And the green loudspeaker,

Hanging to the left of the table

Above the second, high chair,

Hummed to me, barely audible,

For the hundredth time that month,

The tunes one had tired of,

About kalina and malina.

Unexpectedly, on the chair,

Screening the loudspeaker,

There appeared a certain old man

Of the noblest appearance.

A huge and handsome forehead

Was adorned, without a doubt,

Gathering toward the bridge of the nose,

By three deep wrinkles.

Beside the venerable bald crown,

The old man's thick locks

Fell down upon his shoulders

In a white, foam-bright wave.

His gray beard,

Also in locks and ringlets,

Covering his chest to the waist,

Called forth admiration.

His eyes slightly narrowed,

(Nearsightedness, evidently)

He looked at me in silence,

With attention and with love.

By the apparition's appearance

I was not in the least dismayed,

So pleasant and unfrightening

Was his calm aspect.

Still, just in case,

I called to mind King David

And his holy meekness.

( Though in the meekness of King-David

I did not particularly believe,

For the affair with Joab,

And others of the same sort,

Do not exactly smell of meekness.

Probably the chroniclers

Who did not call him meek

He put to death. And willy-nilly

The rest would call

King-David very meek).

The apparition coughed,

And, smoothing its peyos with a hand,

Was evidently about

To enter into conversation with me.

"Moyshe ben reb Avrum-Itskhok"

(Only once in my whole life

Was I named in this way,

In a backwater synagogue,

On the solemn day of bar-mitzvah,

That is, on the day when thirteen

Years of my age were completed

And I was enrolled

In the company of adult Jews).

" Moyshe ben reb Avrum-Itskhok!"

I am your distant, distant forefather

In the direct male line,

Rabbi Zkharya ben reb Zorakh.

And though a thousand generations

Divide the two of us,

Yet by an incomprehensible play

Of the laws of genetics,

You were born in my likeness

By the chromosome of every cell,

And therefore I have appeared

Today to visit you".

Rabbi Zkharya expressed himself

Without the slightest accent,

And even "akaled" a little,

Altogether in the Moscow way,

As if he had grown and been reared

Not in most ancient Palestine,

But in the crooked lanes

By the Arbat or Plyushchikha.

I was pleased that the rabbi

Had not fallen behind life, in paradise,

And, as it seemed, was at home

In the modern sciences too.

And besides, I was flattered

By his words about the resemblance

( I have already said that the rabbi

Was a handsome man).

So that I was filled, toward my forefather,

With the deepest respect,

And, smiling pleasantly,

Listened further with interest.

"I was born in that period

When the tribe of Israel

Languished in the delta of the Nile

At the labors of Pharaoh.

And in my time Rabbeinu Moyshe, -

Moses, the holy teacher,

Managed to lead out of captivity

All the tribes of the Jews.

And today, on the Passover evening,

I shall tell you, my descendant,

Of the exodus of Israel

From distant Egypt".

I was taken aback in confusion,

A chill ran over my skin, -

Can it be that I shall have

To hear the Haggadah again!?!

Can it be that Rabbi Zkharya

Left heaven for this purpose,

To bring an unhappy descendant

Such a torment:

But ben Zorakh smiled:

"Do not be afraid, do not be afraid.

I am not Rabbi ben Akiba, -

I do not seize hold of clasps.

If only it grows boring -

Yawn three times in a row,

And instantly I shall melt away,

Like mist beneath a bright sun"

Such a guarantee

Seemed to me far stronger

Than the guarantees of a frontier

Which were given to the small countries

By the members of the former League of Nations.

Seeing that I had come to believe,

The apparition - Rabbi Zkharya

Sat more comfortably in the chair

And began his flowing tale.

"Time, in a furious torrent

Flows across the universe

And carries off beyond return

Much joy and much grief..."

But while my splendid forefather

Philosophizes a little,

So as to shape an introduction,

As every orator loves to, -

I shall take the moment

To whisper in the reader's ear

A piece of useful advice

For his hard life.

Respected reader,

If you grow bored as you read,

Then, having yawned three times in a row,

Make haste and close the book,

Regardless of the cover.
