Chapter IV

One Sabbath has already passed,

And yet another Sabbath,

And the Jews were still standing

Before Mount Sinai.

once toward evening Rabbi Zkharya,

Having settled by the campfire,

Began to tease Nathan

About the halt at Sinai.

"Well now, honored Reb ben-Ezra,

Where then are your prophecies?

Why do you not lead

Us to the promised land?"

Despite all the spite

And the mockery in Zkharya's speech,

Dvoira's husband showed

Not the least embarrassment.

"If you so wish it,

Respected ben-Zorakh,

I may, to you, as to a friend,

But under the very strictest secrecy,

Explain the motion of the springs

Hidden in the cunning mechanism

Usually called

Political struggle."

"Well. I can swear to you

That I shall be able to keep a secret,

Before men and before gods

Of all times and all peoples."

"Come, come, Rabbi Zkharya, -

Your atheism is known to me!

I do not need your oath -

I will take you at your word.

This little oasis,

At the foothills amid the desert,

Is fortified by nature itself,

As a natural fortress.

Jesus, when he worked

In Pharaoh's general staff,

Knew this place by chance

From stories and from maps.

And so long as there was not ruled out

The unpleasant possibility

That, having learned of the departure

Of Israel from Egypt,

Pharaoh would manage, all the same,

To detach a couple of divisions

From the Ethiopian bridgehead

So as to send them after the Jews, -

Yegoshua and Moisey

Resolved to wait a while

In this fortified place,

So as not again to be exposed

To the risk of meeting on the march,

In the midst of the bare desert,

With a fully battle-ready

Mobile military unit.

In this time Yegoshua

Is to give his soldiers

Combat training,

By robbing, in a fight, the caravans

Passing through the desert,

And by raids laying waste

The defenseless settlements

In the oases round about.

All these measures,

While raising the spirit of the troops

Would at the same time serve

As a source of supply too.

And for the present Rabbi Moyshe,

As a great expert on the desert,

Was to, in the guise of a yogi,

Set out again on reconnaissance,

So as to determine more exactly

Where it would be handier and easier

For the Jews to found

The State of Israel.

Having secretly seen Moyshe off

And taken a heartfelt leave of him,

Navin announced in the morning,

As you yourselves had heard,

That the wisest Rabbi Moyshe,

In the height of Mount Sinai,

Would fast for forty days,

So as by a hunger strike to obtain

A consultation with God

On the question of the further

Advance of Israel

And of the urgent ratification

Of the Jewish constitution."

"Reb Nathan, I beg your pardon,"

Rabbi Zkharya put in a word, -

"It may be that all this is true,

But why must Navin

Hide the truth from the people?"

"Rabbi Zkharya, you are naive," -

Nathan observed from on high, -

"Where, when, what politician

Has told the people the truth?

In this concrete case

Navin acted sensibly;

For, if the Jews

Had learned even for one moment

That the moment now is dangerous,

And that at this time with them

Rabbi Moyshe is not on the spot, -

All would have cried with one voice

That Moisey had fled in disgrace

And left us in the desert,

So as to save his own hide

From Pharaoh's reprisal."

"Yes, perhaps that is how it would have been.

To tell the people the truth,

Sometimes, is incautious" -

Rabbi Zkharya agreed

And with an already habitual gesture

Again touched his earlobe, -

"Rightly it is said in the fairy tales:

As the peoples, so the leaders".

Thus in a friendly conversation,

Every evening they settled

Rabbi Zkharya and Reb Nathan

A number of cardinal questions.

And not only this pair,

But all the other Jews too,

The unaccustomed idleness

After long years of work

Led into conversations

About abstract questions

And, especially, about the temper

And about the outward appearance of God.

For in the Egypt they had left

Busts of God, and portraits,

Could be seen both in the temples

And in the windows of shops.

And the Jews, by inoculation,

But without malicious intentions,

Wanted to see God

Tangible, concrete.

The majority soon decided

That in appearance the Jewish God

Ought to be like a mighty Kerub,

A winged Babylonian bull.

(How, with the passing of centuries,

The winged bull, the dread Kerub,

Gradually turned

Into a kerubin - a cherub,

A little angel with a tender: face, -

I do not intend to decide;

I only look on in amazement

At such a transformation!)

The minority, for its part, supposed

That two wings were too few for God,

And that he looks, most likely,

Like the six-winged Teref. *)

Hundreds of various delegations

Came to Aaron

With a request to make, as soon as could be,

At least some god or other,

Since in this backwater,

After noisy Egypt,

With no work and nothing to do,

It is very dull to live without a god.

(Honored reader!

You have, perhaps, laughed

At this wish - a god

To be obtained for amusement.

But had you, reader,

Spent even half a year

Without newspapers, cinema, magazines,

Books, theaters, a lecture, readings

Without study, without work,

Without civic assignments -

Then you, reader, for yourself

Would have invented a god yourself).

Not seeing in their wish

Any bad motives at all,

Aaron conducted without dispute

Among the Jews who had gathered

A voluntary subscription

For objects of gold,

From which it would have been possible

For them to cast any god whatever.

Since of gold there collected

Only thirty kilograms in all,

(A god is a god, but a bit of gold

Will not come amiss for oneself either)

Then to cast a large bull

Did not prove possible,

Even if they had contrived

To cast it with a hollow middle.

And the caster Nokhum cast,

Putting his art to work,

A small golden

Likable little calf.

Instead of eyes bloodshot,

And a fierce, wild muzzle,

The craftsman, to the calf's little muzzle,

Gave the likeness of a smile,

On an enormous pedestal

Of large granite stones,

The poor little calf

Looked altogether tiny.

But the Jews and the Jewesses,

In the simplicity of their souls,

Were glad even of such

A tangible god.

Even many Jews

Deep in their souls were content

With the small size

Of their own golden calf.

For a little god

Ought to have a smaller appetite

Than in Egypt the enormous

And voracious monsters.

So the sacrifices

Will also be much smaller,

And one need not of one's own will

Give away one's last farthing,

Aaron, having set the god in place,

Showed him to the people,

(To the prayer, from idleness,

All forty thousand appeared/.

And, having lit, for propriety's sake,

A couple of pounds of incense,

So as to be done the sooner,

Muttered a moleben under his breath.

And, having posted beside the god

Two Levites as guards,

He hastened to busy himself again

With matters that would not wait.

But the contented Jews

Did not want to disperse,

And for joy decided

To make a dancing evening.

Having invited the military band

And ten cymbalists,

The young people fell to dancing

On the ground before the god.

And the more solid Jews,

Such as Zkharya and Nathan

Having sat down on the nearest hillock,

Drank foaming beer.

And, unhurriedly carrying on

A heartfelt conversation,

Looked on with pleasure

At the amusements of the young.

Suddenly amid the merry crowd,

Gaunt and shaggy,

With glittering eyes,

Rabbi Moyshe appeared.

Without a word to anyone,

He rushed to the pedestal,

And with a mighty blow of his stick

Knocked the calf from the pedestal.

In amazement the musicians

Dropped their instruments

And the dancing couples

Turned to stone for a moment.

Enraged, Rabbi Moyshe,

Not letting them come to themselves,

With his stout traveling staff

Struck to the right and to the left.

Not having grasped the cause of the anger

Of their beloved prophet,

The astonished Jews

Dispersed to their tents.

Only the couples of young people,

Not wishing to part,

Decided to go on with the dances

On a lawn, at the edge

Of the nearest palm grove.

"What is your view, ben-Ezra", -

So Zkharya asked Nathan, -

Why did Rabbeinu Moyshe

Grow angry with the Jews?"

Dvoira's husband, for the first time in his life,

Did not find an answer at once,

Could only shrug his shoulders

And, in great bewilderment,

Spread his hands wide,

"I cannot understand, ben Zorakh" -

Having thought a moment, he replied, -

I do not see in this anger

Any political sense.

However, perhaps Moisey

Was angered that the Jews

Had not managed to make a god

Of the proper dimensions?

But then why be angry?

Moyshe ought simply

To have ordered copper added

And a large god cast".

Rabbi Zkharya thought a long while,

Shook his head in thought,

Stroked his beard with his hands,

And then said to Nathan:

"I do not think that Moyshe

Thinks as primitively as that;

And, it seems to me, the cause

Lies much deeper here.

For the invisible god

Only Rabbi Moyshe sees,

And, for the time being, this right

He holds as a monopoly.

So, all his orders

And any opinion of his

He can present to us

As issuing from God.

And therefore the calf,

Visible to every Jew,

May undermine the foundations

Of Moyshe's power over the people,"

"What are you saying, Rabbi Zkharya!

Not one of all the Jews

Will want, by so much as a hair,

To limit Moisey's power.

Still, even if you are right,

Then by today's public

Liquidation of the calf

The incident is entirely closed".

"You don't know where Noemi is?" -

Khava broke in on Zkharya, -

Tomorrow I'll give that girl

A good hard boxing of the ears!"

"Only you could !" -

Zkharya growled angrily, -

"I myself allowed Noemi

To go and dance under the palms".

A moonless night came down

Over the oasis asleep,

And it was so dark,

As it is only in the desert.

The campfires had long gone out,

The dogs had fallen asleep by the tents,

And in the murk of the dark night

A calm snoring was heard.

Suddenly a desperate, screeching

Cry, full of the pain of dying,

Having cut through the night's silence,

Plunged into the sky like a dagger.

And, as if at a signal,

At that very instant,

Cries and groans came

From the right, the left, above, below...

Now someone close by cried out,

Rattled with a death rattle,

And fervently begs someone,

Dying, for mercy.

Women's wails, a frantic groan,

Death, striking without warning

Under cover of the black night,

Turned into utter hell

The peaceful camp at Sinai.

Between the wheels of his cart,

Seized by a black horror,

Stopping his ears, Zkharya

Still heard those groans.

And it was not the fear of a possible death

That brought the horror upon Zkharya,

But the absurdity of these ghastly

Unexpected sufferings,

Audible in the terrible, wild howl,

That carried everywhere,

Of voices that had lost their humanity,

Human voices.

And it seemed that this night,

Full of fear and suffering,

Full of cries and torment,

Would never have an end.

Perhaps minutes had passed,

Perhaps an eternity had raced by,

And over the gray desert,

At last, dawn began to glimmer.

And in the faint light of dawn

The Jews suddenly saw

That it was not the warriors of Egypt

And not the spirits of the netherworld,

But the soldiers of Yegoshua,

Bathing up to their knees in blood,

And crazed from the blood,

Stab and hack indiscriminately

Old men, children, and women

And their husbands, unarmed,

Leaving behind them

The corpses of the wounded and the dead

Blood-covered Jews.

Their commander Yegoshua

Under pressure from Moisey

Having agreed to this

Revolting slaughter,

Seeing that day was breaking,

Ordered the bugler the recall

To sound, so that the Jews,

Crazed with fear,

Would not take it into their heads, into the desert,

To scatter like sheep,

Which did not answer at all

To Rabbi Moyshe's intentions.

No sooner had the dawn risen into the sky,

Than heralds everywhere,

Drowning out the weeping and the groans,

Announced to all the Jews

That for their own good

Our great Lord by this

Preventive beating

Showed that he loves them

More than all other peoples,

And how much stronger he is

Than golden, granite, copper

Man-made idols.

Unfortunately, Rabbi Zkharya

Did not hear the announcement,

For he, in terrible anxiety,

Together with Khava and Nathan

Was trying in vain to find

His beloved daughter - Noemi,

Who had not returned by morning

To the place of their encampment.

And none of their acquaintances

Could answer anything

To Zkharya's anxious question:

"Have you not seen Noemi?"

Now the little sun, high,

Has risen in the blue sky,

Already they are tired of searching -

Noemi is not to be found.

Suddenly beyond the camp, under a palm,

By the cooled ashes of a campfire,

Nathan ben-Ezra saw

The body of a girl lying there.

Noemi's white dress,

In dark-brown stains of blood,

Torn open, had laid bare

A terrible wound on the body.

Beside it, fingers clenched,

Her hand was lying: -

It was plain that Noemi

Had managed to raise a rune,

Defending herself by instinct

Against the blow hanging over her, -

As if with a maiden's hand

You could stop a heavy sword!

Khava howled like a wolf in her grief,

Throwing herself at her daughter's corpse,

Upon herself, gone out of her mind,

Tore at her thick hair.

And ripping her clothes,

Clawed her breast with her nails.

Rabbi Zkharya ben Reb Zorakh

An atheist and a callous skeptic

  1. *) Of the outward appearance of the Teref, now called a Seraph, the author has no information, except for A. S. Pushkin's indication that it has six wings for surgery. However, all the Serafimas whom the author had the good fortune to meet in person had an angelic appearance and an even more angelic character.
