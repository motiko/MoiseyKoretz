Letter seven

20. 11.44

My dear little daughter! For some twenty days now I have had no letter from you. By the former norms of our correspondence that would have been little, but over the last few months you and I have somehow grown closer in spirit, and when there is no letter from you for a long time I begin to pine. Generally speaking, of late I have been less hungry, have put on a little weight, and for that reason have come to long much more for freedom and to feel the weight of imprisonment. This is an interesting phenomenon, little studied by psychology, which we here in the camps have often to observe. When a man is pressed to the last edge, all spiritual sufferings and deprivations are wiped away and become strange and incomprehensible. I do not claim that the sufferings of Anna Karenina, which led her to suicide, are not truly tragic experiences, but I am convinced that if she had been deprived of freedom and forced every minute to worry about a crumb of bread, then Vronsky's attitude toward her, and that of high society, would have played far too small a part for her, and thanks to that, although new sufferings would have been added to the old, the very thought of suicide would not have entered her head.

This conclusion is borne out still more by the fact that suicides in the camps are extremely rare, in my opinion even rarer than at liberty.

What a foolish habit, explaining everything logically! I grew lonely for my daughter , so I have to write a whole dissertation on the subject. I simply want very much to see you, my own Irusya! Take this into account and write more often.

But I am in your debt. I wrote at such length in the last letter that I did not manage to answer your questions.

I will try to fill this gap, and since your letter of 9.10.44. is thoroughly businesslike, I too am tuning myself to a businesslike frame.

My dears! ( What I am writing applies equally both to you and to Mama, and so I am switching to the plural). I am not angry with you and I am not offended, and I have no right at all to be offended. I understand clearly what your material situation is, and with bitterness I feel that every rusk you send me you have taken from your own mouth. But you too must understand what a man who smokes goes through when for a month he has nothing to smoke, when he is forced to beg a gulp of smoke at the price of a whole mug of bitter humiliations or, forgetting his own dignity, to crawl under the table collecting cigarette butts, drawing the mockery even of people close to him and losing the place in camp society that he had won with such difficulty. That is why it angered and hurt me so that because of a box you held up for a whole month the parcel that had already been promised and prepared. For several days now I again have nothing to smoke. I think with horror that if up to now, up to your receiving this letter, you have still not sent me something to smoke, that means I have nothing to wait for before 20 December. I beg you, my own ones, make every effort, do not begrudge the trouble, the time and the means, but send me at once, the moment you receive this letter, a parcel with stronger samosad. By the way, if it is a matter of money, there ought to be no hitch over that. Tell me how much samosad costs where you are( only it must be strong), and I will at once transfer to you from my personal account the money for 20-30 glasses of samosad. Well, enough about me. Irochka, I return to your letters. It seems to me, judging by the preliminary information you have sent, that your situation this year is all the same better than last year. Say what you will, 450 labor-days is not 300 labor-days. What probably fills you with pride especially is that of that number a third are your labor-days. It is a pity, of course, that you earned them with your hands and not with your head, but I am sure that the feeling of independence which a person's first earnings bring him, the confidence in himself which it gives, will help you to begin sooner to make use of your head as well.

It is terribly a pity, too, that I cannot go with you, as you remember on my last visit to Leningrad, when you chose yourself a red hat, and dress you as a sixteen-year-old girl would want to be dressed. You have to manage by yourself. Write me what, in the way of clothes and shoes, you have been able to arrange for yourself this autumn. Once I took an interest in women's rags and was not bad at these things. Write in detail what you finally received for the labor-days. How is the health of the piglet of my name ( it is very sweet of Mama, in getting a piglet, to think of me too ). What is the general state of things in your room and in the village.

Write, Irkutka, about everything. Or else I shall be angry.

I kiss you tightly. I am waiting for letters and for something to smoke ( Help, I want a smoke).

MKorets.
