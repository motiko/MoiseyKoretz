Letter eight

22.12.44

My dear little daughter! You kept me waiting a good long while for this letter of yours! Angry I was not, but I missed you very much. With every letter the bond between us grows stronger, and that is the chief joy of my life. Thanks to this correspondence I gain not only a daughter, a person close to me, my own, and loved by me, but also a friend, a young and interesting friend, who is beginning life with trust in me. Claude Farrère has a novel written in epistolary form (that is, consisting of letters). I do not remember the contents of that novel, nor its title; I remembered only that the correspondence is between a young French officer and a young girl from one of the provincial French towns. They became acquainted in the most conventional way, through an advertisement in a newspaper, and never saw each other, but as a result of a year's correspondence the officer fell in love with her and asked her to become his wife. In several letters she refused, but then she agreed.

The officer, who was serving with the units that occupied Constantinople in the years 1918 -19, that is, after the First Imperialist War, takes leave and goes to her. But after the war, mines that had torn loose from their places in the minefields laid during the war still wandered the seas for years. The steamship on which the hero of the novel is traveling strikes one such mine, and the novel ends with a newspaper report of his death. When I write letters to you, I often recall this book. But if you only knew, darling, how I want the end of our correspondence to be not a tragic denouement in the French style, but a happy end (a happy ending), as is the custom in American films. After hundreds of abductions and chases the film ends with a kiss into the diaphragm, that is, directly in front of the lens of the motion-picture camera.

A word, now, about the happy end and about the novel you have read, A Nest of the Gentry. The dissatisfaction which the ending of this novel has stirred in you comes from the mass public's wish to be entertained by a book, to get a pleasant feeling from a happy ending, and nothing more. But you will agree with me that the spur to thought, to the wish to remake life and, above all, oneself, is an outcome true to life, even if a tragic one. Of course, as you yourself guess, I am very glad that you have moved to Ivanovo. I do not have a very high opinion of that city, where I have been two or three times, but however bad it may be, still it is not a village, with its frank, though cheerful, relations among people...

Do you love poems, Irochka? Have you tried writing any yourself, and what came of it? You wrote once that you had begun to keep a diary. I would very much like your letters to me to take the place of a diary for you. If you agree to this, I undertake to return your letters to you regularly, so that they might serve you as a diary.

For a diary is a mirror of one's past self, and people keep one in order to preserve their past for themselves. If you agree to my proposal, your past will be kept for you, and day by day I shall be watching your present. One may object that it is hard to be as frank with another person as with one's own diary, but need you be shy with someone who is thousands of kilometers away from you, who has not seen you in all your conscious life and, perhaps, never will see you...

Let us try, Irushenka. Then I shall probably be able to describe to you, if not your whole character, then at least its intellectual side. NB (Latin - it replaces the tiresome word "by the way", in the sense in which that word is so unsuccessfully used in letters) on character and on certain complications it gives rise to. All this is far from labuda, and it is worth writing about. And the word labuda itself must be struck out of one's usage in the most decisive way.
