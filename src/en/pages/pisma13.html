Letter thirteen

19.12.46 from Inta to Leningrad

My dear Irochka!

I have just received your letter. I am glad that you have made a firm choice of faculty, though I am a little ashamed that one of the chief motives for which you are giving up the physics faculty is the difficulty of getting in. One should never give anything up because of a difficulty, and one must by every effort attain the desired goal. In this case your only justification is that the goal is not really so very desired by you; it is plain from everything that the philological faculty draws you all the same. Well and good, we shall consider this question settled between us. If my plans come true, then it is possible that in the future we may even work together, since my hopes for the future are bound up precisely with literary work. I do not like it that you give up the medal with so light a heart. I am indifferent to these trinkets, but if they exist, one must strive with all one's might to obtain them. Ambition, and the striving to be first, the best, is a splendid quality of a man-fighter. And for the sake of this one must work. It may not succeed, but one must strive. Let us return to the question of the department. Again I feel that you are drawn to the department of West European literature, but because of the difficulties you are giving it up. Of course, the department of German literature is of no interest, but English literature (+ American) is such rich and interesting material that it is simply a sin if, for not knowing the language, one does not go into that department. As it seems to me, medalists are admitted to the university with examinations only in the special subject, in this case in English.

If you manage to obtain at least the silver medal, there is a possibility of preparing in English over the summer. If you take such a decision, then in a thing or two I shall be able to help you. Begin to work in this direction, and further on it will be clear. Together with this letter I am sending you 50 rub. instead of a gift for your coming-of-age day. I wanted to gather more, but it did not work out. I wanted to send this money directly in your name, so as to mark your having grown up, but you never did answer me how you are listed in the documents- Esfir or Irina. Therefore I am sending it in Mama's name. But do tell me about this all the same, since it is possible that toward the end of March I shall again have a chance to send you something. I should like, as I already wrote in the previous letter, that you use this money for the theaters, but you are free to dispose of it as you wish. I turn to the beginning of the letter. It is good that your schoolwork forces you to work intensively. But one must know how to insert into the school program one's own, a non-school one. Judging by your letter, to some extent you do this. You mention Gorky so often in your letter that I too have wanted to write to you about him. There was a time, at about the age of 16, when I was very taken with Gorky, especially his romantic works. This is a mighty talent, with an enormous power of artistic observation and a skill at conveying sharply and in relief, at stamping, the characters of people and the situations of life. But when I grew older and there appeared in me the ability to resist, with the help of my own thoughts and feelings, the overwhelming force of great artists and thinkers, my attitude to Gorky changed. First of all I sharply condemned his Chelkash, Old Woman Izergil, and other works of the same type. frightening in them is the glorification of the bestial anarchism of the heroes. Chelkash, Izergil are beautiful beasts, thoughtlessly and senselessly destroying their own lives and the lives of others, destroying the most precious thing that exists in the world.

And not even for the sake of the struggle for existence, like wolves, but for the sake of senseless impulses that have no justification except unlimited egoism and self-admiration. Repulsive is the scene in Makar Chudra, when the old Gypsy Danila coldly plunges his knife into the back of the youth who killed his daughter, because such is the law of revenge. And all this is written so beautifully so beautifully that instead of feeling a natural revulsion toward these Gypsies and tramps, who stand outside humanism, the highest feeling a person ought to cultivate in himself, you, reading, together with Gorky, begin to admire them, that is, you cultivate the beast in yourself. That is frightening. In his later novels Gorky, with merciless force, shows the black swamp of philistine life. And yet his favorite hero Matvei Kozhemyakin, his heroes, are those people among whom he grew up, the Volga merchants, rising and going to ruin, standing only a span away from those lumpen-heroes of whom he wrote earlier. But here what is already good is that one more often feels revulsion toward them than love ( By the way, ask Shura for the history of her family- great-grandfathers, grandfathers, and father. Typically Gorky material. So beware, in your blood too there is the blood of the Gordeyevs and the Kozhemyakins). But however Gorky blackens them, you still feel that these are his own people, close to him. In My Universities Gorky writes in one place of the wary, hostile attitude of the people around him toward the intelligentsia. Later, with his mind, he drew closer to it; by education and by profession he entered into it; but this unjust hostility, this wariness, remained for his whole life. He feels intellectuals to be alien even when he tries to write well of them (Children of the Sun). And all his hostility he pours out in the three-volume Klim Samgin. It was this novel that served me as the impulse for a new attitude to Gorky. I love the Russian intelligentsia, I love its history, I love its high humanism and its self-sacrifice.

It was she she who created the great Russian literature, its glorious traditions of truthfulness and of a revolutionary spirit. It was she who gave thousands of martyrs and fighters for a better future for mankind, disinterested fighters, sacrificing their own interests for the sake of an idea. Who are Belinsky, Dobrolyubov, Nechaev, Sofya Perovskaya, Dostoevsky, Kropotkin, Bakunin, Lenin, and thousands and thousands of others besides? And the intelligentsia has many faults, which it never concealed. But one cannot thrust forward only those and forget all that is good, as is done in Klim Samgin. This I do not forgive. Gorky seeks a way out in the revolutionary proletariat. But here his heroes are less alive than the merchants and the tramps. Either he makes them close to these two types, or he sets them up on stilts. Tolstoy in Resurrection, though schematically and from a distance, wrote more truthfully about these people, so that Lenin called him the mirror of the Russian revolution. Yet still, over all these tendencies in Gorky there prevails the high artistic truthfulness of a great writer-master. And I am glad that you read a great deal and work on him. Perhaps you will work out your own view of him, and will look at him in another aspect than I do. I should only like this to be your view, worked out by your own mind and feeling, and not simply taken over, even from the cleverest person on earth. Now, as to literacy. For ordinary life your literacy is quite sufficient, but for literary work it is rather too little. The cause of this, it seems to me, is those years you spent in the village school. But in all the rest, the provincialism which I noticed in you with such pain several years ago has been completely erased. On the whole the language of your letters gladdens my parental, literary heart. I do read the newspapers, little daughter; I have even read Margarita Aliger's play Zoya, so that, when you mention such persons, you need not go into explanations of who they are. Though I have fallen behind life, it is not altogether. It matters that one know how to give talks that are not dull, since later this will help in work as a lecturer. And besides, one can love the Russian language not only in written, but also in spoken speech.

Since I now put a great deal of strength into the writing of poems and have chosen this occupation as the chief one for the rest of my days, I am very interested in your attitude to poems, so as to be able to talk with you on this subject too. Do you love poems, have you read many of them, which of the poets of our century do you like best of all, have you read Blok and Gumilyov, Mayakovsky, Yesenin? Whom do you like among the poets who are writing now? Do you have favorite poems, which ones? Write to me about this, Irusha. It seems I once sent you my poem The little birch. How did you like it? Are there boys at your school? Do you meet anyone, and do you make friends? Or has a monastic style of life set in among you now? Do you dance anywhere? I should like to have some idea of what the life of young people is now, of good, thoughtful young people, to whom, as it seems to me, you too belong. Of course you will not write me your secrets; with you girls, one could not drag them out even with pincers, let alone by a letter, but you can hint. This letter will reach you at the beginning of January, and if it is delayed, then just in time for your birthday. 18 years, the end of school, the beginning of the university, the beginning of a full life. It is good, is it not, despite the hunger, the cold, the torn shoes ( by the way, on high heels or still on low ones), the lack of money and of a minimum of outfits, and still it is good. That is why I congratulate you especially warmly on this birthday of yours and kiss you hard, hard. ( You are probably pretty now, are you not?) And when I think that I have a grown-up daughter, then though I ought to feel myself an old man, still I do not feel it, and I think that a dear and beloved comrade has grown up for me. Once more I kiss you, my dear Irochka, Papa Misha.

Shura! I congratulate you on the birthday and the coming of age of our little girl. You have done a great thing, that you were able alone to raise her and to see her through to the end of school. A little longer and it will be easier for you: even if she does not begin to earn, she will still receive a stipend. I envy you that you are with her on this solemn day.

I kiss you, Misha.
