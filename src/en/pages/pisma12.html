Letter twelve

10.11.46. Mezhog Leningrad.

...That you live in a constant whirl is a very good thing. You must train yourself to live in a disciplined way and without a break, that is, to think, to gather knowledge, to give it away, to ivorit, and to be able to win a firm place in your work and among friends. It is not bad to dance, too, to go in for sport, and to do much else. But everything must be done intensely, with the utmost strain of intellect and body. The most frightening thing is lazily lost time, hours after which the soul is left with a feeling of emptiness; the most frightening thing is to be glad that yet another day has gone by. Some poet ( I cannot for the life of me remember) said that in art bad taste is the laziness of the mind of the feelings. And the mind and the feelings can be trained, and must be trained, just as the body is. It is very good that you go to lectures on the history of literature. But Gukovsky and K. Simonov and the other lecturers ( judging by these two ) are intelligent and talented people, able to see sharply and to speak convincingly, and so there is a danger that you will grow used to living by another's mind, by another's perception of so fine and mighty a thing as Russian lyric poetry. Try to read the material yourself before the lecture, try to understand and to shape your own personal perception of the lyric, argue inwardly with the lecturers, and give way only when he has laid you on both shoulder blades.

Working in a drama circle is not bad; it helps one to understand better and to take in the theater and the images it gives. But regular rehearsals are an immense waste of time, justified only when the occupation is professional, or when there is nowhere to put that time ( the worst thing) or when the rehearsals are only a pretext for meetings with one or another of the people who interest you. In any case, it is an expensive, but meaningful amusement. I liked how you write that in material terms things are a little hard for you. That beautiful restraint, especially when in fact it is not a little hard but desperately hard. I picture this clearly to myself, and, to tell the truth, I do not entirely understand how you can live at all just now on Mama's earnings alone. I was very glad when today I got a letter from my papa in which he reports that he has transferred three hundred rubles to you. And on the other hand my little daughter too, while all of this ought to rest on me. But unfortunately I am helpless for the present. It grieves me very much that you do not go to the theaters. Hard as it is for me, I will scrape together a small sum for your coming of age, and send it to you specially for the theaters. I remember how at 17, in Moscow, in a torn blouse and, often, with an empty stomach, I was a constant visitor to the galleries ( the last seats in them) of all the Moscow theaters. And the pleasure I got was far greater than that of the visitors to the stalls and the boxes. One may deny oneself anything, but one cannot give up the theater. ( By the way, what is your name and patronymic in the documents. According to the birth certificate – Esfir Moiseevna, is it still so?) Your despair over the room does not touch me at all. In that very room I lived my last three student years with my wife and one small, rather loud-voiced little being, Irochka. The conditions were a bit worse than yours, since I had both to study and to earn a living, and I wanted to fuss over you too; not for nothing were you called papa's girl.

And I was 21-23; lectures, theaters, books and music were no less interesting to me than they are to you now, you may be sure. But it never once entered my head that one could fall into despair over all this, or demand less of oneself. And only today, after your letter, did I notice for the first time that I had had bad conditions for study in my student years. And ask Mama in what conditions we lived and studied before that. Let her tell you about Galyanovka, where she and I lived in Moscow at the start of my studies ( remember, Shura, beyond the Yauza). And you fall into despair over this room in Leningrad ( Mama and I lived for many years in a walk-through room with a window that looked out onto a wall). And losing heart is not a thing you and I are supposed to do at all. In my student life I lost heart only once: when you were about to be born. It seemed to me that with a child in my arms I would not be able to get through by any path ( at 19 that is truly frightening., I advise you not to follow my example). And still I got through. And you will too. And once you had been born, I never regretted it: I liked you far too much. Well, enough of that. Let us take up the more important question of the institute. Since you do not like drafting, it is not worth going to a technical institute. That drops away at once. Therefore, the university ( we set medicine aside, since, I see, it does not draw you at all). You do not mention singing; of music you write that you understand nothing in it ( that is a pity). As far as I understand you, what remains for you is the choice between the physics faculty and the literature faculty. And there we shall stop. I love literature passionately. I began writing poems at the age of 7. From the age of 18 I published a little. Then I dropped it and took up physics. After a 9-year break, when I wrote only now and then, from the age of 27 I came to Moscow again and took up literary work in earnest ( Shura does not know about this, since we were very far apart at that time). This time I wrote popular-science things and quickly, in a single year, won a comparatively decent place in the world of the popular magazines of Moscow. I was on the editorial boards of the magazines Technology for Youth and I Want to Know Everything. I published quite a lot.

At the full gallop of my unfolding literary career they took me to the nail. Taken to that same nail, I returned to verse. For a long time I wrote it only now and then, in a dilettante way. But from the very beginning of this year I took it up in earnest and reached a success that surprised me myself. Then I noted with bitterness that, from a lack of faith in myself, I had gone the wrong way, overlooking my chief calling, poetry. But even five minutes before death one must not give up the struggle and life. And I took, at 38 years of age, cut off from people and from literature, with no real prospects of getting on, a decision which may seem to you and to any sober person ridiculous and absurd: I decided to change the profession of a physicist for the profession of a man of letters. The world does not care in the least about my decision; to it I am N so-and-so, used as an X-ray technician and a microbiologist ( a good thing it is not as a ditchdigger). Neither my physics nor my literature is of any use to it. But I do not care in the least about its opinion, and I work like a slave, putting into this work all my soul, all the force of my intellect, and everything I have thought through and felt through. And I will bring it about that the world does care about my work. But that is about me, and it does not matter. And for all that, if I were offered the choice of the faculty which would best prepare me for literary work, I would again choose the physics one, the faculty at which Turgenev studied and which Herzen finished. Only the physics faculty, only the study of physics, can give a clear and distinct idea of the world, and the habit of analyzing one's thoughts and observations honestly, distinctly, and to the end. Especially if one takes into account the enormous future which the appearance of atomic energy opens before physics. Thus if you have a creative calling, if you want to be a writer, then on no account go to the literature faculty. In such schools they can grind smooth the brightest talent. It is another matter if what interests you is not the making of literature, but the study of it. Then you must go to the philology faculty.

I advise you then to choose the English language and its literature. After Russian literature, which I place above all the rest, Anglo-American literature is the mightiest contingent of world literature. Here not only study and comparison are possible, but active creation in the form of literary translation. But I must warn you very seriously, and you ought to guess it yourself, that on the path of the philologist and the literary scholar there is many times more than on the path of the physicist, and that they find far more fault with the former. Think about this more deeply. In general, you must choose yourself, by your calling, but I would be glad if you chose physics. There is little drafting there, and it is not frightening. After all, I was not a bad student, and to this day I cannot draft at all. Let us go on, Irusha, with our discussion of this in our further letters. And now I will move on one more page of your letter. I love music very much and I know how to feel it. I learned to love it and to understand it late, already living in Leningrad, in that very room which now drives you to despair. But opera I never could come to love. And it seems to me that I understand why. Of all the arts, music can express human feelings and moods the most vividly and richly. Into what is musical one can put a thought. But with music one cannot in any way express an event. And when a musical commentator wants to persuade me that a certain passage of Tchaikovsky's symphony Ansar ( the 2nd symphony) expresses how a kite wants to seize a doe and how Ansar drives the kite off with a spear, I can hear that passage, but I find it absurd even to think that this very event is what the music expresses, and no other. The mood cast by this event is of course expressed, but one can pick dozens of other events, of an entirely different order, that fit this same mood. But the composer wants the listener to receive precisely that combination which called the music up in him. And so he grabs the word by the collar, crumples it, mangles it, and forces it to serve the music.

Words have a music of their own, deeply expressive, bound to their meaning. If that were not so, one could not write poems, in which the sound of the words and their meaning fuse into a single magical music. Only for that reason do poems exist; otherwise everything in literature would be expressed in prose. See how exactly, in a good poem, the sounds are placed, what a gigantic part their repetition-rhyme plays. And in opera they are distorted without mercy. Listen to how distinctly the word тревога sounds, how the consonants distinctly convey its tense meaning. And in opera they make of it тре-ево-о-га-а-а-а, and the word becomes pale, tormented, inexpressive. Does the music gain from this union, so tormenting to the word? Hardly. Instead of the broad range of personal feelings and thoughts which mighty music can call up in you, you are forced to follow the cropped thought of the libretto. And how free music can act is described, vividly to the point of pain, by Lev Tolstoy in The Kreutzer Sonata and in many places in Hoffmann ( see at least The Musical Sufferings of Kapellmeister Johannes Kreisler ). That is why I do not love opera. And I am sorry for the novel War and Peace. Judging by your description of Prokofiev's opera, they have hollowed out of it all its great content, leaving only the love- lyric intrigue and a little of the historical events. Thank you for the description. From it I clearly pictured the whole production. I ask you from now on, too, to spoil me sometimes with reviews of what you have seen. this is useful for you as well, and for me it is unusually interesting. The 9 years I have been cut off from the theater have not put out my love for it. Well, I have run on. For three hours now I have been writing you this letter, my own girl. But I am not bored, and if not for a sense of measure I would have covered much more paper. I did not manage to write anything practical about myself. But that does not matter either. Still, I did manage to write to you about one of the most important factors of my present life, about that work to which I give every free minute I have. I kiss you tightly, my grown-up little daughter.

Your papa Misha.
