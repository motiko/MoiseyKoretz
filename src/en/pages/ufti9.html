Pyatigorsky’s statement

NKVD of the Ukrainian SSR

Statement of L. M. Pyatigorsky

At the end of June of this year I was on my way to a lecture at the university. A foreign specialist, the engineer Weissberg, stopped me and began a conversation with me about how he had been unfairly dismissed from OSGO. He then suggested that I come into a café, bought pastries, and turned the conversation to the situation at UFTI. He described that situation as follows: the scientific level of the institute is falling, the institute is headed for ruin. As the reasons he put forward that Gey and other Party members, who are not prepared for scientific work, are dragging the institute down, lowering its level to their own. In his opinion, the director Davidovich is destroying the institute. Weissberg also hinted to me that, in his opinion, Comrade Gey had "wheedled" the special work in Moscow. The way out of the situation that had arisen, as he would have had it, was to have the Party organization at UFTI led by workers, and a "tough fellow" as secretary. Along the way one must train cadres of Communists — scientific workers. Weissberg named no concrete measures in my presence. I expressed disagreement with the claim that Communists are dragging UFTI down. As proof that this is a lie, I cited Garber and Komarov, who, in my view, are fully abreast of the scientific interests of the institute. At that our conversation ended. Weissberg is thoroughly oriented in the political situation in the USSR and often makes entirely firm, correct statements. I therefore could not take his conversation with me for a political error, and I began to watch Weissberg more closely. What struck me was that he has long conversations with many members of the UFTI staff, walking with them along the asphalt path in the courtyard. In particular he was, as it seems to me, working on Party members, the assistant to the director of the institute, Comrade Kravchenko, having several lengthy conversations with him. From some of the staff I heard that he said to them, of the worker graduate students (Communists), that it was no good at all. Gradually I began to form the impression that Weissberg is creating an atmosphere of squabbling at UFTI, organizing an atmosphere of rumors, gossip, and a hostile attitude toward the Party organization and the directorate. At the end of June I brought my suspicions to the attention of Comrade Zalivadny.

A number of foreigners work at our UFTI. From Tisza, a staff member of the theoretical section, I more than once heard "news" of a "squabble" unfolding at UFTI, and in most cases, when I asked where he was picking all this up, he answered me that he had heard it all from Vaiselberg (also a foreigner; he does not work, but he lives at UFTI). Vaiselberg is a friend of Weissberg's. I recommended that he feed less on rumors, but he answered me that he learns them willy-nilly, since he has supper at Vaiselberg's. All this says once more that Weissberg is at the center or, in any case, close to the center of a group of people who need a squabble. An active part in this group was taken by (a foreign woman) Varvara Ruhemann. From Landau I heard that (a foreigner) Houtermans had set the life span of our UFTI at 2 months. He said, in scientific language, that this means only that if everything goes on as it is going, the institute will not last more than 2 months. Thus V. Ruhemann, Houtermans, and Tisza, who explained his discontent as purely bad relations with Davidovich, are oriented toward Weissberg.

Korets was reluctant to enter into conversations with me. In the group of people who organize the squabble (Weissberg, Korets, Landau, Shubnikov, V. Ruhemann) he played, in my opinion, a prominent role. He was called the organizer of a positive program. Thus, allegedly in a critical article in the wall newspaper, he discredited two staff members of the special laboratory, members of the Komsomol committee, and held them up as an example of the wrong salary policy at the institute.

At Landau's I saw a list of staff members with their salaries given, and against each one there was also entered the salary that ought to be paid. I think that the whole list and the whole idea of putting salaries in order are in the spirit of Korets's article and according to the idea of Korets himself. Korets himself received a salary wholly unsuited to his work at UFTI. Korets had an extremely bad influence on Landau. They were on friendly terms, spent a great deal of time together, and a change in Landau for the worse was plainly visible after Korets's arrival at the institute. Instead of carrying out the training of cadres in an organized way, Korets pushed Landau onto a path of adventurism, of a break with the Party, of independent actions, and in the end he always had in mind that the struggle with Davidovich was a matter of "who—whom" and that, if one lost the pace, Davidovich would destroy the institute. I think that most of the harmful ideas belong to Korets (a new salary estimate, dividing the institute into two parts, if we do not eat up Davidovich, then Davidovich will eat us up, the idea of forming a bloc with the foreigners, and others). In any case, he always speaks very demagogically, with confidence, always making it seem that whoever does not agree with him wishes the institute to perish. At the Komsomol committee Korets admitted that he had concealed his social origin, and the next day Landau again insisted that he had not concealed it. At the same time he convinced Landau that Landau's leaving his job would reflect harmfully on Korets. Such duplicity is a characteristic trait of Korets. I am sure that Korets stands at the center of the squabble. I do not think that he alone could have played the most harmful, disorganizing role at UFTI, corrupting the staff, setting himself and his line against the line of the Party organization, creating an unhealthy atmosphere around the Party organization and the Komsomol, and thereby carrying on work that is harmful in practice.

Landau very often started conversations with me about the ways of the institute. He told me that the institute is headed for ruin, that Davidovich is leading the institute to collapse. He told me that the special work greatly lowers the level of the institute, that he had wheedled the special work in Moscow in order to win favor and to load the institute with special work. In his opinion, there is a part of the staff at the institute - Gey, Zalivadny, Muzkansky and others, headed by Davidovich, who stand to gain if the institute goes downhill - they will not then be thrown out.

Landau several times told me that salaries at the institute are set up wrongly.., that "the slutskins are not literate in physics and that is a fact, and yet they are considered physicists." In general, a tendency of that kind to isolate certain employees (the special lab) from the institute. Landau urged me, for example, that the institute had to be divided into two parts - Slutskin's laboratory together with the nuclear laboratory separately and, besides that, separately "UFTI proper," made up of Shubnikov, Obreimov and Landau. Landau told me that the special work at UFTI would fail, since it had been entrusted not to real physicists and the scientific supervisors had not been brought in. In general it came out like this: look at what they are doing – they take on special work, this must not be done. And if one is to take it, then it must be entrusted to the scientific supervisors and, at the same time, there was talk of a lowering of the level of the special work. These conversations at first went, of course, along the line of criticism of Davidovich, and at the time, in conversation with Landau, I shared his opinion that Davidovich is tactless, but against his talk of the special work and the special workers I put up a firm resistance. Later, when I began to notice in Landau's conversations the same thing that Weissberg had said, I firmly advised him to act through the Party organization and not to embark on adventures. But Korets had a strong influence on Landau, and Landau would not listen to anyone. In my opinion, the path of the anti-Soviet conversations of which I have spoken is one Landau takes thanks to Korets. I told Komarov that Landau smells of Weissberg.

Landau, for his part, said that the removal of Davidovich is the first stage of the struggle for the development of physics in the Soviet Union. The plan he sketched was roughly this:

1) To obtain the removal of Davidovich;

2) To strengthen UFTI as a scientific unit;

3) To make UFTI a center for the training of cadres.

Landau called Korets an organizer, since Korets had supposedly offered him a number of valuable suggestions on how to train cadres. It seems to me that at bottom Landau did not want to follow an anti-Soviet line, but, having drawn close to Korets, he went along a path hostile to the Party.

The outright hounding of Strelnikov as a scientific worker, the fact that they did not want to confirm him as a candidate of sciences at the time when Brilliantov was confirmed, once again underlines the political bent of the grouping that organized the squabble. I often said to Landau: "You are against Strelnikov, so why was Brilliantov confirmed?" He would say then that, supposedly, a mistake had been made, that Brilliantov had been confirmed. When I spoke of all this with Landau, he declared to me: "so what, am I hounding Communists, or something?" I could not object to him in anything, for this would have meant accusing my own scientific supervisor of an anti-Soviet act, and at the institute the Party organization was cautious about a political characterization of Landau.

Personally I have known Landau since 1932, from the time he came to UFTI. It seemed to me all along that he was drawing ever closer to the Party and to Party views, but that was so only until Korets began to influence him.

In closing I should like to note that, in my opinion, what is going on at UFTI cannot simply be called a mistake; as I said at the time to Zalivadny and to Komarov, and as became still clearer to me afterward, the squabble at UFTI was only a screen, a method that was a component part of a broader adventure.
